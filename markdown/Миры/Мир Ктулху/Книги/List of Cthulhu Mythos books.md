# List of Cthulhu Mythos books

Many [fictional works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictional_book "Fictional book") of arcane literature appear in [H. P. Lovecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft")'s cycle of interconnected works often known as the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"). The main literary purpose of these works is to explain how characters within the tales come by [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") or [esoterica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_esotericism "Western esotericism") (knowledge that is unknown to the general populace). However, in some cases the works themselves serve as an important plot device. For example, in [Robert Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bloch "Robert Bloch")'s tale "[The Shambler from the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shambler_from_the_Stars "The Shambler from the Stars")", characters inadvertently cast a spell from the arcane book _[De Vermis Mysteriis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Vermis_Mysteriis "De Vermis Mysteriis")_.

Another purpose of these fictional works was to give members of the [Lovecraft Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft") a means to pay homage to one another. Consequently, [Clark Ashton Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_Ashton_Smith "Clark Ashton Smith") used Lovecraft's _[Necronomicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necronomicon "Necronomicon")_ (his most prominent creation) in Smith's tale "Ubbo-Sathla". Likewise, Lovecraft used [Robert E. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Howard "Robert E. Howard")'s _[Nameless Cults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nameless_Cults "Nameless Cults")_ in his tale "Out of the Aeons". Thereafter, these fictional works and others appear in the stories of numerous other Mythos authors (some of whom have added their own [grimoires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimoire "Grimoire") to the literary arcana), including [August Derleth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Derleth "August Derleth"), [Lin Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Carter "Lin Carter"), [Brian Lumley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Lumley "Brian Lumley"), [Jonathan L. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_L._Howard "Jonathan L. Howard"), and [Ramsey Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey_Campbell "Ramsey Campbell").

## B

### Book of Azathoth

> He must meet the Black Man, and go with them all to the throne of [Azathoth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azathoth "Azathoth") at the centre of ultimate Chaos. That was what she said. He must sign in his own blood the book of Azathoth and take a new secret name now that his independent delvings had gone so far.  
> —H. P. Lovecraft, "[The Dreams in the Witch House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dreams_in_the_Witch_House "The Dreams in the Witch House")"

The _Book of Azathoth_ is a creation of Lovecraft's. It is mentioned in "The Dreams in the Witch House" as a book harbored by [Nyarlathotep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nyarlathotep "Nyarlathotep") in the form of the Black Man (or [Satan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satan "Satan")). The protagonist, Walter Gilman, is forced to sign the book in his blood, pledging his soul to the Other Gods. The idea of this fictional book is likely based on classical descriptions of witch-cults, [Satanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satanism "Satanism") rites, and the signing away of souls.

Other authors have expanded on the _Book of Azathoth_. [Michael Alan Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Alan_Nelson "Michael Alan Nelson") writes (in his _Fall of Cthulhu_ series for [Boom! Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boom!_Studios "Boom! Studios")) that the signer attracts the attention of the Other Gods by writing their name in the book. Glynn Owen Barrass states (in _The Starry Wisdom Library_) that the _Book of Azathoth_ praises the Lovecraftian pantheon and renounces/mocks the Christian scripture.

### Book of Eibon

> . . . _The Book of Eibon_, that strangest and rarest of occult forgotten volumes ... is said to have come down through a series of manifold translations from a prehistoric original written in the lost language of [Hyperborea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperborean_cycle "Hyperborean cycle").  
> —Clark Ashton Smith, "Ubbo-Sathla"

The concept of _Book of Eibon_, or _Liber Ivonis_ or _Livre d'Eibon_, is attributed to [Clark Ashton Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clark_Ashton_Smith "Clark Ashton Smith") and can be said to be his equivalent of Lovecraft's _Necronomicon_.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-dailydead2017-1) It appears in a number of Lovecraft's stories, such as "[The Haunter of the Dark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Haunter_of_the_Dark "The Haunter of the Dark")" (_Liber Ivonis_), "[The Dreams in the Witch House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dreams_in_the_Witch_House "The Dreams in the Witch House")" (_Book of Eibon_), "[The Horror in the Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Horror_in_the_Museum "The Horror in the Museum")" (_Book of Eibon_), "[The Shadow Out of Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shadow_Out_of_Time "The Shadow Out of Time")" (_Book of Eibon_) and "The Man of Stone", a collaboration with [Hazel Heald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazel_Heald "Hazel Heald") (_Book of Eibon_).

Within these narratives, this book is supposed to have been written by [Eibon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eibon "Eibon"), a [wizard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wizard_\(fantasy\) "Wizard (fantasy)") in the land of Hyperborea. It was an immense text of arcane knowledge that contained, among other things, a detailed account of Eibon's exploits, including his journeys to the Vale of Pnath and the planet Shaggai, his veneration rituals of [Zhothaqquah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhothaqquah "Zhothaqquah") (Eibon's patron deity), and his magical formulae—such as for the slaying of certain otherworldly horrors. In the lore of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"), only one complete fragment of the original is known to exist, scattered in different places of our world, though there are translations in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language "English language"), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language"), and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin")—_Liber Ivonis_ is the title of the Latin translation.[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-2)

Smith presents his short story "The Coming of the White Worm" as Chapter IX of the _Book of Eibon_.[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-3)

[Lin Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Carter "Lin Carter") wrote numerous "completions" or imitations of Clark Ashton Smith stories which purported to be various sections of the _Book of Eibon_.

Outside of Smith's and Lovecraft's mythoses, the book notably appears in [Lucio Fulci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucio_Fulci "Lucio Fulci")'s [supernatural horror film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural_horror_film "Supernatural horror film") _[The Beyond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beyond_\(1981_film\) "The Beyond (1981 film)")_ (1981), where inappropriate use of it opened up one of the seven gates of Hell, allowing its zombie-like denizens to cross over.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-dailydead2017-1)

### Book of Iod

The _Book of Iod_ was created by [Henry Kuttner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kuttner "Henry Kuttner") and first appeared in his short story "Bells of Horror" (as Keith Hammond; [1939](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_in_literature "1939 in literature")). The original _Book of Iod_ is written in the "Ancient Tongue", possibly a combination of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language "Greek language") and [Coptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_language "Coptic language"). While its origin is unknown within the narrative, the _Book of Iod_ may have been written by the mysterious author "Khut-Nah", which sounds remarkably like Kuttner. The _Book of Iod_ contains details about Iod, the Shining Hunter, Vorvados, and [Zuchequon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Old_One_compendium#Zushakon "Great Old One compendium"). According to the lore of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"), the [Huntington Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huntington_Library "Huntington Library") of [San Marino, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Marino,_California "San Marino, California") is said to hold an expurgated translation, possibly in [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin"), by Johann Negus.[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-4)

_[The Book of Iod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Iod "The Book of Iod")_ was also the title of a short-story collection published by [Chaosium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaosium "Chaosium") in 1995, containing 10 Cthulhu Mythos stories by Henry Kuttner, along with three related stories by Kuttner, [Robert Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bloch "Robert Bloch"), [Lin Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Carter "Lin Carter"), and [Robert M. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Price "Robert M. Price").

## C

### Celaeno Fragments

The Celaeno Fragments is credited to August Derleth. In his [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel") _The Trail of Cthulhu_, "Celaeno" refers to a distant planet that contains a huge library of alien literature. The character Professor [Laban Shrewsbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Shrewsbury,_Laban "Cthulhu Mythos biographies") and his companions traveled to Celaeno several times to escape Cthulhu's minions. Later in the lore's timeline, Shrewsbury wrote the Celaeno Fragments, a transcript of what he remembered of his translations of the books in the Great Library of Celaeno. He submitted the transcript, which consisted of about 50 pages, to the Miskatonic University's library in 1915.

### Cthäat Aquadingen

The _Cthäat Aquadingen_, possibly meaning _Things of the Water_ (as _aquadingen_ can be translated from Dutch as "water/aqua things"), was created by [Brian Lumley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Lumley "Brian Lumley") for his short story "The Cyprus Shell" ([1968](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1968_in_literature "1968 in literature")). This fictional work, by an unnamed author, deals with [Cthulhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu "Cthulhu") and other sea-horrors, such as [Inpesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_deities#Table_of_Great_Old_Ones "Cthulhu Mythos deities"). It also contains many so-called _Sathlattae_, rituals and spells related to [Ubbo-Sathla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_God#Ubbo-Sathla "Outer God"). It is first mentioned as appearing in northern [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany "Germany") around 400 AD. According the lore of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"), a [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") version was written between the 11th and 12th century, as was an English translation that appeared sometime in the 14th century.

### Cultes des Goules

_Cultes des Goules_, or _Cults of Ghouls_, was created by [Robert Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bloch "Robert Bloch") (August Derleth claimed to have invented the fictional work, but this was denied by both Lovecraft and Bloch himself).[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-5) The work is often misattributed to August Derleth because the fictional author is the "Comte d'Erlette".[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-6) It is a book on [black magic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_magic "Black magic") and the uses of the dead written by the character Francois-Honore Balfour ([Comte d'Erlette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#d'Erlette,_Comte "Cthulhu Mythos biographies")) in 1702 of the lore's timeline. It was first published in [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") and later denounced by the church. Only a handful of copies are exist in present day settings. One of the established copies was kept for 91 years in an arcane library of the [Church of Starry Wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_cults#Church_of_Starry_Wisdom "Cthulhu Mythos cults") in [Providence, Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Providence,_Rhode_Island "Providence, Rhode Island"). After [Robert Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Harrison_Blake "Robert Harrison Blake")’s mysterious death in 1935, [Doctor Dexter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Dexter,_\(Doctor\)_Ambrose "Cthulhu Mythos biographies") removed the grimoire and added it to his library.

_Cultes des Goules_ is mentioned numerous times in the works of [Caitlin R. Kiernan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caitlin_R._Kiernan "Caitlin R. Kiernan") and plays an especially important role in her [2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2003_in_literature "2003 in literature") novel _Low Red Moon_. The text is also prominently mentioned in her short story "Spindleshanks (New Orleans, 1956)"—collected in _[To Charles Fort, With Love](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/To_Charles_Fort,_With_Love "To Charles Fort, With Love")_ ([2005](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2005_in_literature "2005 in literature")).

The book _Cultes des Goules_ is also mentioned in passing as being part of a collection that was discovered in the titular castle in the 1981 novel _[The Keep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Keep_\(Wilson_novel\) "The Keep (Wilson novel)")_, but does not appear in the [1983 movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Keep_\(film\) "The Keep (film)") based on the book.

## D

### De Vermis Mysteriis

Main article: [De Vermis Mysteriis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Vermis_Mysteriis "De Vermis Mysteriis")

_De Vermis Mysteriis_, or _Mysteries of the Worm_, was created by [Robert Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bloch "Robert Bloch"), first appearing in Bloch's [short story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story") 'The Secret in the Tomb" (_Weird Tales,_ May 1935)[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-7) and featured extensively in Bloch's "The Shambler from the Stars" ([1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_in_literature "1935 in literature")). The fictional [grimoire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grimoire "Grimoire") was used by [Stephen King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_King "Stephen King") in his short story "[Jerusalem's Lot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem's_Lot "Jerusalem's Lot")" and novel _[Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revival_\(novel\) "Revival (novel)")_.

### Dhol Chants

The _Dhol Chants_ was first mentioned in the short story "The Horror In The Museum" ([1932](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1932_in_literature "1932 in literature")) by Lovecraft and Hazel Heald. They are alluded to in passing as a semi-mythical collection of chants attributed to the almost-human people of [Leng](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leng_\(plateau\) "Leng (plateau)"). The chants themselves are never described, nor do they appear in any other of Lovecraft's works. August Derleth later used the chants in his stories "The Gable Window" ([1957](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1957_in_literature "1957 in literature")), _[The Lurker at the Threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lurker_at_the_Threshold "The Lurker at the Threshold")_ ([1945](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1945_in_literature "1945 in literature")), and "The Shadow Out of Space" (1957).

According the lore of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"), Miskatonic University's library is said to hold a copy of the _Dhol Chants_.

## E

### Eltdown Shards

Richard F. Searight invented _The Eltdown Shards_ in a head-note (which purported to be a quotation from this text) to his story "The Sealed Casket" (Weird Tales, March 1935). The story was actually published in that issue without the headnote. Lovecraft later quoted the unpublished headnote in a letter to Clark Ashton Smith, "leading some to believe that he wrote it".[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-8) He cited the book in _[The Shadow Out of Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shadow_Out_of_Time "The Shadow Out of Time")_ and _[The Challenge from Beyond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Challenge_from_Beyond?action=edit&redlink=1 "The Challenge from Beyond (page does not exist)")_.

The Eltdown Shards are mentioned in numerous mythos stories as mysterious pottery fragments found in 1882 and named after the place where they were discovered, Eltdown in southern [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England "England"). In the lore timeline, the shards date to the [Triassic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triassic "Triassic") period and are covered with strange symbols thought to be untranslatable. Nonetheless, several characters penned their own interpretations of the markings, including Gordon Whitney and his _The Eltdown Shards: A Partial Translation_. Many of these fictional works, as well as a number of non-academic versions, are mentioned in stories featuring secretive cults.

Whitney's translation is similar to the [Pnakotic Manuscripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pnakotic_Manuscripts "Pnakotic Manuscripts"), a fictional text produced by the [Great Race of Yith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Race_of_Yith "Great Race of Yith"). The translation describes Yith, the planet from which the Great Race came, and the Great Race's fateful encounter with the Yekubians. A magical formula from the 19th shard is for the summoning of the "Warder of Knowledge"; unfortunately, the dismissal portion of the ritual is garbled, so the summoning of this being could prove calamitous. Despite its connections to the Great Race, lore of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos") has established that the Eltdown Shards were most likely inscribed by the [Elder Things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_Thing "Elder Thing"), who probably buried the ceramics in England when it was part of the great supercontinent [Pangaea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pangaea "Pangaea").[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-9)

## G

### G'harne Fragments

The G'harne Fragments first appeared in the works of [Brian Lumley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Lumley "Brian Lumley"). They are described as a set of miraculously preserved shards of [obsidian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obsidian "Obsidian") or some other black stone that record the history of the pre-human [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Africa "Africa") city of G'harne. Within the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"), the lost city is located somewhere in the southern [Sahara Desert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sahara_Desert "Sahara Desert"), and is currently a frequent haunt of the [chthonians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chthonian_\(Cthulhu_Mythos\)?action=edit&redlink=1 "Chthonian (Cthulhu Mythos) (page does not exist)").

The characters responsible for translating of the fragments are [Sir Amery Wendy-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Wendy-Smith,_Sir_Amery "Cthulhu Mythos biographies") and Gordon Walmsley. Both of these scholars died in Lumley's works: Sir Amery in "Cement Surroundings" ([1969](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1969_in_literature "1969 in literature")) and Walmsley in "In the Vaults Beneath" ([1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_in_literature "1971 in literature")).

## K

### The King in Yellow

Main article: [The King in Yellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_King_in_Yellow "The King in Yellow")

A creation of [Robert W. Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_W._Chambers "Robert W. Chambers"), _The King in Yellow_ is a [play](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Play_\(theater\) "Play (theater)") featured in a collection of short stories also named _The King in Yellow_, published in [1895](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1895_in_literature "1895 in literature").

According to the stories, the play was widely censored. The author is unknown, and is believed to have committed [suicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide "Suicide") after publishing it in 1889. The play is named after a mysterious supernatural figure featured in it, who is connected to a peculiar alien symbol, usually wrought in gold, called the [Yellow Sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_Sign "Yellow Sign"). Though the first act is said to be "innocent", all who read the play's second act either go mad or suffer another terrible fate. Its setting and events include mysterious places and entities such as [Carcosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcosa "Carcosa"), [Hastur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hastur "Hastur"), and the [Lake of Hali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carcosa#Associated_names "Carcosa"), names that Chambers borrowed from the writings of [Ambrose Bierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambrose_Bierce "Ambrose Bierce").

Lovecraft was a fan of the book and included references to the Lake of Hali and the Yellow Sign in his short story "[The Whisperer in Darkness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Whisperer_in_Darkness "The Whisperer in Darkness")" ([1930](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1930_in_literature "1930 in literature")). August Derleth later expanded on this connection in his own stories, rendering Hastur as an evil deity related to Cthulhu and the King In Yellow as one of his [incarnations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation "Incarnation"). [Karl Edward Wagner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Edward_Wagner "Karl Edward Wagner") and [Joseph S. Pulver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_S._Pulver "Joseph S. Pulver") returned Chambers creations to their original cosmic horror roots. Both are great advocates of Chambers' work and have written many stories that utilize Chambers creations. Pulver also edited an anthology of Chambers inspired stories called _A Season in Carcosa_.

### Necronomicon

The _[Necronomicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necronomicon "Necronomicon")_ is arguably the most famous (or infamous) of Lovecraft's fictional works. It appears in a number of Lovecraft's stories, as well as in the writings of other authors.

## O

### On the Sending Out of the Soul

_On the Sending Out of the Soul_ appears in [Henry Kuttner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kuttner "Henry Kuttner")'s short story "Hydra" ([1939](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1939_in_literature "1939 in literature")). It is described as an eight-page pamphlet on [astral projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astral_projection "Astral projection"). The pamphlet appeared in [Salem, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem,_Massachusetts "Salem, Massachusetts"), in 1783 of the lore's timeline and circulated among [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") groups. Most copies were destroyed in the wake of a series of grisly murders.

The first seven pages of the pamphlet contain vague mystic writing; however, the eighth page details a formula for effecting astral travel. Among the required ingredients are a brazier and the drug _[Cannabis indica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannabis_indica "Cannabis indica")_. The formula is always successful but has an unforeseen side effect: it invokes the horrid [Outer God the Hydra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Kuttner_deities#The_Hydra "Henry Kuttner deities").[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-10)[_[page needed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Citing_sources "Wikipedia:Citing sources")_]

## P

### Parchments of Pnom

The Parchments of Pnom is a manuscript written by [Hyperborea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperborean_cycle#Hyperborea "Hyperborean cycle")'s leading [genealogist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genealogy "Genealogy") and soothsayer. It is written in the "Elder Script" of that land and contains a detailed account of the lineage of the Hyperborean gods, most notably [Tsathoggua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsathoggua "Tsathoggua").

### Pnakotic Manuscripts

The Pnakotic Manuscripts were created by [H. P. Lovecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft") and first appeared in his [short story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story") "[Polaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris_\(short_story\) "Polaris (short story)")". They are noteworthy for being the first of Lovecraft's [fictional arcane books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_arcane_literature "Cthulhu Mythos arcane literature").[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-11) They were named after the place where it was kept, the city of Pnakotus, a primordial metropolis built by the [Great Race of Yith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Race_of_Yith "Great Race of Yith"). The Great Race is credited with authoring the Manuscripts, though other scribes would add to it over the ages. According to Lovecraft's story "[The Other Gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Other_Gods "The Other Gods")", the Pnakotic Manuscripts originated in "frozen Lomar", a region in the [Dreamlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreamlands "Dreamlands").

[F. Paul Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._Paul_Wilson "F. Paul Wilson") is among the authors who have referred to this collection in their own work; a collated version of the Manuscripts appears in Wilson's novel _[The Keep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Keep_\(F._Paul_Wilson_novel\) "The Keep (F. Paul Wilson novel)")_.

### Poakotic Fragments

### Ponape Scripture

The _Ponape Scripture_ first appeared in [Lin Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lin_Carter "Lin Carter")'s short story "Out of the Ages" ([1975](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_in_literature "1975 in literature")). The _Scripture_ is a [manuscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manuscript "Manuscript") found in the [Caroline Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Islands "Caroline Islands") by Captain [Abner Exekiel Hoag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Hoag,_\(Captain\)_Abner_Exekiel "Cthulhu Mythos biographies") sometime around 1734. The book showed signs of great age—its pages were made of palm leaves and its binding was of an ancient, now-extinct [cycadean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycad "Cycad") wood. It was written in [Naacal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naacal "Naacal") (the language of [Mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_\(Cthulhu_Mythos\) "Mu (Cthulhu Mythos)")) and appears to have been authored by Imash-Mo, high priest of [Ghatanothoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghatanothoa "Ghatanothoa"), and his successors. The book contains details of Mu and of [Zanthu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Zanthu "Cthulhu Mythos biographies"), high priest of [Ythogtha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xothic_legend_cycle#Ythogtha "Xothic legend cycle"). With the help of his servant Yogash (hinted to be a [Deep One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_One "Deep One") hybrid[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-12)), Hoag managed to write a translation of the manuscript. But when he tried to have it published, his efforts were thwarted by religious leaders who strongly objected to the book's references to [Dagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dagon_\(short_story\) "Dagon (short story)"). Nonetheless, copies of the _Scripture_ have circulated among secretive [cults](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_cults "Cthulhu Mythos cults") (such as the [Esoteric Order of Dagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Esoteric_Order_of_Dagon "Esoteric Order of Dagon")) and other [occult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occult "Occult") groups. After Hoag's death, his granddaughter, Beverly Hoag Adams, published an expurgated version of the book.

In contemporary times, other versions of the _Ponape Scripture_ have seen print. [Harold Hadley Copeland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Copeland,_Harold_Hadley "Cthulhu Mythos biographies"), a leading authority on the _Scripture_, produced a translation of the book, published in [1907](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1907_in_literature "1907 in literature") by [Miskatonic University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miskatonic_University "Miskatonic University") Press. Copeland also cited the book in his work _The Prehistoric Pacific in Light of the 'Ponape Scripture_ ([1911](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1911_in_literature "1911 in literature")). The original version of the manuscript remains at the [Kester Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kester_Library?action=edit&redlink=1 "Kester Library (page does not exist)") in [Salem, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salem,_Massachusetts "Salem, Massachusetts").[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-13)

## R

### Las Reglas de Ruina

_Las Reglas de Ruina_ (literally "the Rules of Ruin") first appeared in [Joseph S. Pulver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_S._Pulver "Joseph S. Pulver")'s [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel") _Nightmare's Disciple_. It is a tome written by Philip of Navarre in 1520, a Spanish friar of the 16th century. The book has been translated in English by Professors Theodore Hayward Gates and Pascal Chevillion in 1714 and describes the Great Old One **Kassogtha**, sister and incestuous bride of [Cthulhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu "Cthulhu"). The book also foretells of the coming of a messiah of destruction, who would be born in the western land of the red savage across the great ocean in Columbus' New World, a man that shall set the Great Old One free from her stellar prison. [Livia Llewellyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livia_Llewellyn "Livia Llewellyn") elaborated on this, describing the violent sexual acts committed by Kassogtha worshipers.

### Revelations of Gla'aki

The _Revelations of Gla'aki_ first appeared in [Ramsey Campbell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsey_Campbell "Ramsey Campbell")'s short story "The Inhabitant of the Lake" ([1964](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_in_literature "1964 in literature")). It was written by a fictional [undead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undead "Undead") cult worshipping the [Great Old One](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Old_One "Great Old One") [Gla'aki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gla'aki?action=edit&redlink=1 "Gla'aki (page does not exist)"). Within the lore of the Cthulu Mythos, whenever Gla'aki slept, the members of his cult had periods of free will, and, since they were part of Gla'aki and shared his memories, they wrote down what they remembered of their master's thoughts. The cult's handwritten manuscripts later came to be known as the _Revelations of Gla'aki_. The text originally contained 11 volumes, nine in the carefully abridged published edition, but the story implies it may have had more at different times in the past.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-14) Rumor has it that Mythos Scholar, Antonius Quine, once published a corrected edition of the Revelations of Gla'aki bound in a single volume.[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-15)

## S

### Seven Cryptical Books of Hsan

The _Seven Cryptical Books of Hsan_ is a collection of writings mentioned by Lovecraft in "The Other Gods" (1921) and "[The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dream-Quest_of_Unknown_Kadath "The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath")" (1926). In both stories, the books are mentioned in conjunction with the Pnakotic Manuscripts. They are kept in the temple of the [Elder Ones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elder_God_\(Cthulhu_Mythos\) "Elder God (Cthulhu Mythos)") in the city of [Ulthar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulthar "Ulthar"); these are the only copies existing in the current day of Lovecraft's stories. The character [Barzai the Wise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Barzai_\(the_Wise\) "Cthulhu Mythos biographies") studied the books before his journey to see the gods dancing on Mount Hatheg-Kla, while another character, [Randolph Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph_Carter "Randolph Carter"), consulted them during his quest to reach [Kadath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kadath "Kadath").

The collection can be considered to be an analogue to the _[I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching "I Ching")_, a Chinese text of cosmology and divination.

## T

### Testaments of Carnamagos

> Now, as he sat there in a state half terror, half stupor, his eyes were drawn to the wizard volume before him: the writings of that evil sage and seer, Carnamagos, which had been recovered a thousand years ago from some Graeco-Bactrian tomb, and transcribed by an apostate monk in the original Greek, in the blood of an incubus-begotten monster. In that volume were the chronicles of great sorcerers of old, and the histories of demons earthly and ultra-cosmic, and the veritable spells by which the demons could be called up and controlled and dismissed.  
> —Clark Ashton Smith, "The Treader of the Dust"

_The Testaments of Carnamagos_ was created by Clark Ashton Smith and first appeared in his short story "[Xeethra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xeethra "Xeethra")" ([1934](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1934_in_literature "1934 in literature")). The text is featured more prominently in Smith's "The Treader of the Dust" ([1935](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1935_in_literature "1935 in literature")). Confusedly, Xeethra is set in the far distant future on Zothique, Earth's last continent, whereas "The Treader of the Dust" is set in (Smith's) current times.

The book gives a description of the Great Old One [Quachil Uttaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Old_One_compendium#Quachil_Uttaus "Great Old One compendium"), among others. Only two copies are known of, though one was destroyed during the [Spanish Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition "Spanish Inquisition"). The only remaining copy is bound in [shagreen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shagreen "Shagreen"), and fastened with hasps of human bone.

## U

### Unaussprechlichen Kulten

Main article: [Unaussprechlichen Kulten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unaussprechlichen_Kulten "Unaussprechlichen Kulten")

_Unaussprechlichen Kulten_ was created by [Robert E. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Howard "Robert E. Howard"), and was ascribed to the fictional [Friedrich von Junzt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos_biographies#Von_Junzt,_Friedrich_Wilheim "Cthulhu Mythos biographies"). Howard originally called the fictional book _Nameless Cults_, but both Lovecraft and Derleth gave it the German title which can translate to either **Unspeakable Cults** or **Unpronounceable Cults** (both meaning of the word are in common usage).

The name is grammatically incorrect. In proper German it would be named either _Unaussprechliche Kulte_ or _Von Unaussprechlichen Kulten_ (Of Unspeakable Cults).

## Z

### Zanthu Tablets

The Zanthu Tablets first appeared in "The Dweller in the Tomb" ([1971](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1971_in_literature "1971 in literature")), by Lin Carter. The centerpiece of the story is the discovery of the tablets, which are an important part of Carter's [Xothic legend cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xothic_legend_cycle "Xothic legend cycle").

The tablets themselves are described as 12 engraved pieces of black [jade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jade "Jade") inscribed by the fictional author Zanthu, a wizard and high priest of Ythogtha. They are written in a hieratic form of [Naacal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naacal "Naacal"), the language of the fictional sunken continent of [Mu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu_\(Cthulhu_Mythos\) "Mu (Cthulhu Mythos)"). The tablets reveal a partial history of Mu, describing Zanthu's struggle against the rising cult of [Ghatanothoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghatanothoa "Ghatanothoa") and his own religion's lamented decline. He also describes his failed attempt to release the god Ythogtha from its prison. Upon witnessing three black, beaked, slimy heads, "vaster than any mountain", rising from a gorge, he flees in terror when he realizes that they are merely the god's fingertips. According to Zanthu, he and some of his people escaped the destruction of Mu, which was sunk by the wrath of the Elder Gods.

The character Harold Hadley Copeland published a brochure entitled _The Zanthu Tablets: A Conjectural Translation_ in 1916 of the lore timeline. He made the rough translation using a key borrowed from the estate of another character, Colonel Churchward, the last qualified translator of ancient [Naacal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naacal "Naacal"), and heavily edited it out of a concern for "public sanity". The controversial brochure was later denounced by the academic community and was suppressed by the authorities. Published versions Copeland's later manuscripts have not appeared in any Cthulhu Mythos stories. According to current lore, ten years after the publication of the brochure, Copeland died in an asylum.

The following backstory is provided for how Copeland found the tablets. In 1913 of the lore timeline, guided by the _Ponape Script_, the character Copeland led an expedition into [Indochina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indochina "Indochina") to locate the plateau of Tsang and to find the tomb of Zanthu. After the other members of the expedition died or deserted him, Copeland pressed on, eventually reaching his goal. Opening the tomb, he was horrified to discover that the mummified face of Zanthu resembled his own. Later wandering into a Mongolian outpost, a starving and raving Copeland was the only survivor of the expedition.

Carter's story "The Thing in the Pit" in his _Lost Worlds_ purports to be a translation from the Zanthu Tablets.

## Miscellaneous books

The following is a list of **miscellaneous books**—both real and fictitious—appearing in the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"). Along with the use of arcane literature, texts which innately possess supernatural powers or effects, there is also a strong tradition of [fictional works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fictional_book "Fictional book") or fictionalizing real works in the Mythos. The main literary purpose of books in the Mythos is to explain how characters within the tales come by occult or esoteric knowledge that is unknown to the general populace. However, in some cases the works themselves serve as important plot devices or simply opportunities for members of the [Lovecraft Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft") to pay homage to one another and other sources.

The following table[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Cthulhu_Mythos_books#cite_note-16) is organized as follows:

- _Title_. The title of the work as it appears in the Cthulhu Mythos.
- _Fict/Real_. Fictitious works are denoted by **F**; real-life works by **R**.
- _Author_. The person or character credited as the author of the work. Authors of nonfictional works are real people. If the author is fictitious, the name of the writer who created the work appears in parentheses after the character's name. Surnames of Mythos writers are as follows:
    - Derleth = [August Derleth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Derleth "August Derleth")
    - Bloch = [Robert Bloch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Bloch "Robert Bloch")
    - Howard = [Robert E. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Howard "Robert E. Howard")
    - Lovecraft = [H. P. Lovecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._P._Lovecraft "H. P. Lovecraft")
- _Notes_. A brief summary of the work.
