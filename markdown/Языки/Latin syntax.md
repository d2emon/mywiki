# Latin syntax

**Latin syntax** is the part of [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin "Latin") grammar that covers such matters as word order, the use of cases, tenses and [moods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_mood "Grammatical mood"), and the construction of simple and compound sentences, also known as _periods_.[[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-1)[[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-2)

The study of Latin [syntax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntax "Syntax") in a systematic way was particularly a feature of the late 19th century, especially in Germany. For example, in the 3rd edition of _[Gildersleeve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basil_Lanneau_Gildersleeve "Basil Lanneau Gildersleeve")'s Latin Grammar_ (1895), the reviser, Gonzalez Lodge, mentions 38 scholars whose works have been used in its revision; of these 31 wrote in German, five in English and two in French. (The English scholars include [Roby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_John_Roby "Henry John Roby") and [Lindsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_Lindsay "Wallace Lindsay")).

In the twentieth century, the German tradition was continued with the publication of two very comprehensive grammars: the _Ausführliche Grammatik der lateinischen Sprache_ by Raphael Kühner and Karl Stegmann (1912, first edition 1879), and the _Lateinische Grammatik_ by Manu Leumann, J.B. Hofmann, and Anton Szantyr (revised edition Munich 1977, first edition 1926). Among works published in English may be mentioned E.C. Woodcock's _A New Latin Syntax_ (1959). More recently, taking advantage of computerised texts, three major works have been published on Latin word order, one by the American scholars Andrew Devine and Laurence Stephens (2006),[[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-3) and two (adopting a different approach) by the Czech scholar Olga Spevak (2010 and 2014).[[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-4)

## Latin word order

Main article: [Latin word order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_word_order "Latin word order")

Latin word order is relatively free. The verb may be found at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end of a sentence; an adjective may precede or follow its noun (__vir bonus__ or __bonus vir__ both mean 'a good man');[[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-5) and a genitive may precede or follow its noun ('the enemies' camp' can be both __hostium castra__ and __castra hostium__; the latter is more common).[[6]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-6) There are also stylistic differences between Latin authors; for example, while [Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") always writes __castra pōnit__ 'he sets up camp', [Livy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Livy "Livy") more often writes __pōnit castra__.[[7]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-7)

There are however certain constraints; for example, in prose a monosyllabic preposition such as _in_ 'in' generally precedes its noun (e.g. __in Italiā__ 'in Italy').[[8]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-8) Moreover, even though adjectives can both precede and follow the noun, there is a tendency for different kinds of adjectives to take different positions; for example adjectives of size usually come before the noun[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:0-9)[[10]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-10) (__magnā vōce__ 'in a loud voice', rarely __vōce magnā__), while "modifiers that are more important than their noun or that specify it"[[11]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-11) (e.g. __Via Appia__ 'the Appian Way') usually follow it.

To explain Latin word order there are two main schools of thought. One, represented by Devine and Stephens (2006), argues from the point of view of [generative grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_grammar "Generative grammar"), and maintains that Latin prose has a basic underlying "neutral" word order, from which authors deviate for reasons of emphasis, topicalisation, rhythm, and so on. According to Devine and Stephens, the basic order in broad scope focus sentences is as follows:[[12]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-12)

- Subject – Direct Object – Indirect Object / Oblique Argument – Adjunct – Goal or Source Argument – Non-Referential Direct Object – Verb

The other approach, represented by Panhuis (1982) and Olga Spevak (2010), examines Latin word order from the point of view of [functional grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_theories_of_grammar "Functional theories of grammar"). Rejecting the idea that there is a basic word order, this approach seeks to explain word order in terms of pragmatic factors, such as topic and focus, and semantic ones (1st person before 2nd, human before animals or things, agent before patient, etc.).[[13]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-13)

### Examples of word order

Further information: [Hyperbaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbaton "Hyperbaton")

The order of words is often chosen according to the emphasis required by the author. One way of emphasising a word is to reverse the usual order.[[14]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-14) For example, in the opening sentence of [Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar")'s _Gallic War_, the usual order[[9]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:0-9) of numeral and noun _trīs partīs_ 'three parts' is reversed to emphasise the number "three":

- __Gallia est omnis dīvīsa in partes tres__[[15]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-15)

'Gaul, considered as a whole, is divided into three (parts)'

Another technique used by Latin authors is to separate a phrase and put another word or phrase in the middle, for example:

- __magnam enim sēcum pecūniam portābat__[[16]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-16)

'for he was carrying with him a large sum of money'

The technical term for this kind of separation is "[hyperbaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbaton "Hyperbaton")" (Greek for 'stepping over'); it is described by Devine and Stephens as 'perhaps the most distinctively alien feature of Latin word order'.[[17]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-17)

Placing the verb at or near the beginning of a clause sometimes indicates that the action is sudden or unexpected:[[18]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-18)

- _statim complūrēs cum tēlīs in hunc faciunt dē locō superiōre impetum_[[19]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-19)

'immediately several men, (armed) with weapons, launch an attack on my client from higher ground'

Splitting up an adjective-noun phrase and bringing the adjective to the beginning of the sentence can highlight it. In the following example from Cicero, the splitting of __cruentum__ 'blood-stained' and __pugiōnem__ 'dagger' creates a dramatic effect:[[20]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-20)

- __statim cruentum altē tollēns Brūtus pugiōnem Cicerōnem nōminātim exclāmāvit__[[21]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-21)

'immediately, raising high the blood-stained dagger, Brutus shouted out "Cicero" by name'

Considerations of rhythm and elegance also play a part in Latin word order.[[22]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-22) For example, [Pliny the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Younger "Pliny the Younger") begins a letter as follows:

- __magnum prōventum poētārum annus hic attulit__[[23]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-23)

'it is a great crop of poets this year has brought'

In this sentence, the object (__magnum prōventum poētārum__ 'a great crop of poets') has been brought forward to highlight it. The other striking feature is the order __annus hic__ for the more usual __hic annus__ 'this year'. Two reasons which might be suggested are Pliny's fondness for ending a sentence with the rhythm − u − − u −[[24]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-24) and also no doubt because of the elegant [assonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assonance "Assonance") of the vowels _a-u-i a-u-i_ in the last three words.

## Gender and number

### Gender and number agreement

Latin has three genders (masculine, feminine, and neuter) and two numbers (singular and plural). Pronouns, adjectives, participles, and the numbers one to three have to agree in gender and number with the noun they refer to:

- Masculine : __hic est fīlius meus__:[[25]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-25) 'this is my son'
- Feminine : __haec est fīlia mea__ : 'this is my daughter'
- Neuter : __hoc est corpus meum__:[[26]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-26) 'this is my body'

The same three genders are also found in the plural:

- Masculine : __hī sunt fīliī meī__ : 'these are my sons'
- Feminine : __hae sunt fīliae meae__ : 'these are my daughters'
- Neuter : __haec mea sunt__:[[27]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-27) 'these things are mine'

In Latin, words referring to males are always masculine, words referring to females are usually feminine.[[28]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-28) (An exception is __scortum__ (neuter) 'a whore'.) Words referring to things can be any of the three genders, for example __mōns__ 'mountain' (masculine), __arbor__ 'tree' (feminine), __nōmen__ 'name' (neuter). However, there are certain rules; for example, nouns with the suffixes _-a_ (unless referring to men), _-tiō_, _-tās_ are feminine; the names of trees, islands, and countries, such as __pīnus__ 'pine', __Cyprus__ 'Cyprus', and __Aegyptus__ 'Egypt' are also usually feminine, and so on. Some nouns such as __parēns__ 'parent' can vary between masculine and feminine and are called of "common" gender.[[29]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-29)

When words of different genders are combined, the adjective is usually masculine if referring to people, neuter if referring to things:[[30]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-30)

- __patēr mihī et mātēr mortuī (sunt)__ (Terence)[[31]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-31)

'my father and mother are dead (_masc._)'

- __mūrus et porta dē caelō tācta erant__ (Livy)[[32]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-32)

'the wall (_masc._) and gate (_fem._) had been struck (_neut. pl._) by lightning' (lit. 'touched from the sky')

However, sometimes the adjective may agree with the nearest noun.

## Latin cases

Further information: [Latin declension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_declension "Latin declension")

Nouns, pronouns, and adjectives in Latin change their endings according to their function in the sentence. The different endings are called different "cases". Case endings of a similar kind are also found in other languages, such as Ancient and Modern Greek, German, Russian, Hungarian, Finnish, Sanskrit, Armenian, Classical Arabic, and Turkish.[[33]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-33)

The six cases most commonly used in Latin and their main meanings are given below. The cases are presented here in the order Nom, Voc, Acc, Gen, Dat, Abl, which has been used in Britain and countries influenced by Britain ever since the publication of [Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Hall_Kennedy "Benjamin Hall Kennedy")'s Latin Primer in the 19th century.[[34]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-34) A different order – Nom, Gen, Dat, Acc, Voc, Abl, or its variation Nom, Gen, Dat, Acc, Abl, Voc – is used in many European countries and the United States.

- Nominative : _rēx_ : 'the/a king' (Subject, or Complement (e.g. 'he is the king'))
- Vocative : _rēx!_ : 'o king!'
- Accusative : _rēgem_ : 'the king' (Object, or Goal)
- Genitive : _rēgis_ : 'of the king'
- Dative : _rēgī_ : 'to the king', 'for that king'
- Ablative : _rēge_ : 'with the king' (also 'by, from, in')

(A small line, called a macron, over a vowel indicates that it is pronounced long.)

Another case is the locative, which is used mostly with the names of cities (e.g. _Rōmae_ 'in Rome') and a very limited number of ordinary nouns (e.g. _domī_ 'at home').

### Examples of case use

The following examples from [Caesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julius_Caesar "Julius Caesar") show the cases used in a basic sense:

- _Caesar ... mīlitibus signum dedit_[[35]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-35)

'Caesar (Nom) gave a signal (Acc.) to the soldiers (Dat)'

Here _Caesar_ is the subject of the sentence, and so [nominative case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative_case "Nominative case"); _mīlitibus_ 'to the soldiers' is [dative case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dative_case "Dative case"), a case typically used with the verb _dō_ 'I give' (hence the name "dative"); while _signum_ is the direct object, and so accusative case.

- _Cūriō Mārcium Uticam nāvibus praemittit_[[36]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-36)

'Curio (Nom.) sends ahead Marcius (Acc.) to Utica (Acc.) with the ships (Abl.)'

Here _Cūriō_ as subject of the verb is nominative, _Mārcium_ as direct object is accusative; _Uticam_ is also accusative as it is the goal or object of motion; and _nāvibus_ 'with the ships' has the ablative ending. Although the ending _-ibus_ is the same for both dative and ablative plural, the ablative meaning "with" is more appropriate in this context.

- _Pompeius ... Lūceriā proficīscitur Canusium_[[37]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-37)

'Pompey (Nom) from Luceria (Abl) sets out to Canusium (Acc)'

Here _Pompeius_ is subject (Nom.), _Lūceriā_ shows another meaning of the ablative ending, namely 'from', and _Canusium_ is again accusative of goal. With names of cities there is no need to add a preposition such as _ad_ 'to', but the accusative case alone indicates "to".

An example illustrating the genitive case is the following:

- _(hostēs) ad castra Caesaris omnibus cōpiīs contendērunt_[[38]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-38)

'the enemy (Nom.) hastened towards Caesar's (Gen.) camp (Acc.) with all their forces (Abl.)'

Here _castra_, the goal of motion, is in the accusative following the preposition _ad_ 'to' or 'towards'; _Caesaris_ 'of Caesar' or 'Caesar's' is in the genitive case; and _omnibus cōpiīs_ 'with all their forces' is in the ablative case, with the meaning 'with'.

### Idioms using the dative case

The description of the use of cases is not always straightforward. The classification of the uses of the dative alone takes up nearly twelve pages in Woodcock's _A New Latin Syntax_[[39]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-39) and ten pages in Gildersleeve and Lodge.[[40]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-40) For example, when asking someone's name, a Roman would say:

- _quid est tibī nōmen?_ ([Plautus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plautus "Plautus"))[[41]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-41)

'what's your name?' (lit. 'what is for you the name?')

This is an example of the dative of possession, as in:

- _illī ... duae fuēre fīliae_ (Plautus)[[42]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-42)

'he had two daughters' (lit. 'to him there were two daughters')

Another idiomatic use is the "dative of the person affected":

- _nihil equidem tibī abstulī_ (Plautus)[[43]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-43)

'I haven't stolen anything from you' (lit. 'for you'; compare German: _Ich hab dir nichts gestohlen_)

The dative is also used with verbs of fighting with someone:[[44]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-44)

- _nōlī pugnāre duōbus_ (Catullus)[[45]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-45)

'don't fight with (lit. 'for') two people at once'

Another idiom is the "predicative dative" used with the verb 'to be' in phrases such as __ūsuī esse__ 'to be of use', __labōrī esse__ 'to be a trouble (to someone)':[[46]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-46)

- _nēminī meus adventus labōrī aut sūmptuī ... fuit_ (Cicero)[[47]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-47)

'my arrival was a trouble or expense for no one'

Many verbs which in English take a direct object are used in Latin intransitively with a dative noun or pronoun, e.g. __persuādeō__ 'I persuade', __crēdō__ 'I believe', __resistō__ 'I resist'.[[48]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-48)

- _nōn persuāsit illī_ (Seneca)[[49]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-49)

'he did not persuade him (lit. 'for him')'

- _imperāvit eī_ (Nepos)[[50]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-50)

'he ordered him' ('gave an order to him')

### Prepositions

Frequently, to make the meaning more precise, a noun in the accusative or ablative is preceded by a preposition such as _in_ 'in, into', _ad_ 'to', _cum_ 'with', or _ex_ 'out of'. This is especially so if the noun refers to a person. For example:

- _ad rēgem_ (Acc) 'to the king' (used with a verb of motion such as 'goes' or 'sends')
- _ā rēge_ (Abl.) 'by the king', 'from the king'
- _cum eō_ (Abl.) 'with him'
- _ex urbe_ (Abl.) 'from/out of the city'

However, when the meaning of an accusative or an ablative is clear (for example _Canusium_ (Acc) 'to Canusium', _nāvibus_ (Abl) 'with the ships', _posterō diē_ (Abl) 'on the following day'), the case ending alone is sufficient to give the meaning. Unlike in Greek, prepositions are not used in Latin with the dative or genitive.

#### Prepositions with accusative or ablative

Four prepositions can be followed by more than one case (very similar to usage of these and other prepositions in German), depending on their meaning. These are _in_ 'in' (Abl), 'into' (Acc.); _sub_ 'under' (Abl.), 'to the foot of' (Acc.); _super_ 'over, above' (Acc.), 'concerning' (Abl.); and _subter_ 'under' (usually with Acc.)[[51]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-51)

- _in urbem_ (Acc) 'into the city'
- _in urbe_ (Abl) 'in the city'

#### Position of prepositions

Prepositions almost always precede their noun or pronoun, except that _cum_ 'with' follows a personal pronoun, e.g. _mēcum_ 'with me' and sometimes a relative pronoun (_quīcum_, _quōcum_ and _cum quō_ are all possible for 'with whom').[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:2-52) There are occasional exceptions, especially with two-syllable prepositions after pronouns,[[52]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:2-52) e.g. _haec inter_ (Virgil)[[53]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-53) 'in the midst of these'.

Sometimes when the noun has an adjective it is placed before the preposition for emphasis, e.g. _magnā cum cūrā_ 'with great care' (Cicero),[[54]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-54) but this is not an invariable rule.[[55]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-55) Occasionally also the opposite order (noun-preposition-adjective) may be used in poetry and later prose,[[56]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-56) e.g. _silvā lupus in Sabīnā_ (Horace)[[57]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-57) 'a wolf in the Sabine forest', or _metū in magnō_ (Livy) 'in great fear'.[[58]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-58)

## Latin tenses

Further information: [Latin tenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_tenses "Latin tenses") and [Latin conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_conjugation "Latin conjugation")

Latin has six main tenses in the indicative mood, which are illustrated below using the verb __facere__ 'to make' or 'to do':

- Present : _faciō_ : 'I do', 'I am doing'
- Future : _faciam_ (2nd person _faciēs_): 'I will do', 'I will be doing'
- Imperfect : _faciēbam_ : 'I was doing', 'I used to do', 'I began to do'
- Perfect : _fēcī_ : 'I did', 'I have done'
- Future Perfect : _fēcerō_ : 'I will have done'
- Pluperfect : _fēceram_ : 'I had done'

The verb __sum__ 'I am', which is irregular, has the tenses __sum__, __erō__, __eram__, __fuī__, __fuerō__, __fueram__. Some verbs (conjugations 1 and 2) instead of the Future _-am_, _-ēs_, _-et_ etc. have a different future ending in _-bō_, _-bis_, _-bit_, e.g. _amābō_ 'I will love'.

To these six ordinary tenses may be added various "periphrastic" tenses, made from a participle and part of the verb _sum_ 'I am', such as __factūrus eram__ 'I was about to do'.[[59]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-59)

For the most part these tenses are used in a fairly straightforward way; however, there are certain idiomatic uses that may be noted.[[60]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-60) Note in particular that the Latin perfect tense combines the English simple past ("I did") with the present perfect ("I have done") into a single form; this can make the perfect verb "feel" like it is set in the present ("Now I have done (it)") for the purpose of grammatical sequence of tenses.

## Passive and deponent verbs

### Passive and deponent tenses

In addition to the [active voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_voice "Active voice") tenses listed above, Latin has a set of passive voice tenses as follows:

- Present : __capior__ : 'I am captured', 'I am being captured' (by someone or something)
- Future : __capiar__ (2nd singular __capiēre__ or __capiēris__) : 'I will be captured'
- Imperfect : __capiēbar__ : 'I was being captured', 'I used to be captured'
- Perfect : __captus sum__ : 'I was captured', 'I have been captured'
- Future Perfect : __captus erō__ : 'I will have been captured'
- Pluperfect : __captus eram__ : 'I had been captured'

The three perfect tenses (Perfect, Future Perfect, and Pluperfect) are formed using the perfect participle together with part of the verb __sum__ 'I am'. The ending of the participle changes according to the gender and number of the subject: __captus est__ 'he or it was captured'; __capta est__ 'she or it was captured'; __captī sunt__ 'they were captured', and so on.

Deponent verbs have exactly the same form as passive verbs except that the meaning is active, not passive:

- Present : __ingredior__ : 'I enter', 'I am entering'
- Future : __ingrediar__ (2nd singular __ingrediēre__ or __ingrediēris__) : 'I will enter'
- Imperfect : __ingrediēbar__ : 'I was entering, 'I used to enter'
- Perfect : __ingressus sum__ : 'I entered', 'I have entered'
- Future Perfect : __ingressus erō__ : 'I will have entered'
- Pluperfect : __ingressus eram__ : 'I had entered'

### The use of passive verbs

A passive verb is generally used when it is unnecessary to indicate who did the action:

- _[ille] sē hostibus obtulit atque interfectus est._[[61]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-61)

'he offered himself to the enemy and was killed'

An intransitive verb can also be made passive, provided it is used impersonally in the neuter singular:

- _ubi ad rādīcēs montium ... ventum est, signum extemplō datur_ (Livy)[[62]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-62)

'when (the army) reached the foot of the mountains, the signal was given at once'

When it is desired to show the agent or person(s) by whom the action was done, Latin uses the preposition _ab_ or _ā_ with the ablative case:

- _arx ab hostibus capta est_ (Livy)[[63]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-63)

'the citadel has been captured by the enemy!'

When the agent is not a person but a thing, no preposition is used, but simply the ablative case:

- _corripitur flammīs ... tellūs_ (Ovid)[[64]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-64)

'the earth is seized by flames (i.e. catches fire)'

### Passive of "give"

In Latin, unlike English, only the direct object (not the indirect object) of an active verb can be made the subject of a passive verb.[[65]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-65) It is not correct to say in Latin 'the soldiers were being given their pay' but only 'pay was being given to the soldiers':

- __mīlitibus stīpendium (dabātur)__ (Livy)[[66]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-66)

'pay was being given to the soldiers'

### Impersonal passive

Another unusual feature of Latin, compared with English, is that [intransitive verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intransitive_verb "Intransitive verb") such as __eō__ 'I go', __veniō__ 'I come', __pugnō__ 'I fight' and __persuādeō__ (+ dative) 'I persuade' can be made passive, but only in a 3rd person singular impersonal form:[[67]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-67)

- __ītur in antīquam silvam__ (Virgil)[[68]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-68)

'they go into an ancient forest' (lit. 'going is done')

- __septimō diē Carthāginem ventum est__ (Livy)[[69]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-69)

'on the seventh day they reached Carthage'

- __persuāsum erat Cluviō ut mentīrētur__ (Cicero)[[70]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-70)

'Cluvius had been persuaded to lie' (literally: 'it had been persuaded to Cluvius that he should lie')

### Passive infinitive

The infinitive of a passive verb ends in _-ī_ (3rd conjugation) or _-rī_ (other conjugations): __capī__ 'to be captured, __audīrī__ 'to be heard', etc.

- __in vincula dūcī iubet__ (Livy)[[71]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-71)

'he ordered him to be put in chains'

- __sī vīs amārī, amā__ (Seneca)[[72]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-72)

'if you wish to be loved, love'

The Perfect passive has an infinitive __captus esse__ 'to have been captured', and there is also a rarely used Future passive infinitive made using the supine (__captum__) plus the passive infinitive __īrī__: __captum īrī__ 'to be going to be captured'. It is typically used in indirect statements:

- __occīsum īrī ab ipsō Milōne videō__ (Cicero)[[73]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-73)

'I can see that he is going to get killed by Milo himself'

### Deponent verbs

Most of the verbs ending in _-or_ are true passives in meaning (i.e. they represent actions which are done _by someone_ or _by something_). However, there are a few which are ambivalent and can be either active or passive in meaning, such as __vertor__ 'I turn' (intransitive) or 'I am turned', __volvor__ 'I revolve' (intransitive) or 'I am rolled':

- __vertitur intereā caelum et ruit Ōceanō nox__ (Virgil)[[74]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-74)

'meanwhile the sky turns and night falls upon the Ocean'

In addition, there are a few verbs such as __proficīscor__ 'I set out', __polliceor__ 'I promise', __cōnor__ 'I try' which despite their passive endings have an active meaning. These verbs (which have no active counterpart) are called [deponent verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deponent_verb "Deponent verb"):[[75]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-75)

- __ipse in Italiam profectus est__ (Caesar)[[76]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-76)

'he himself set out for Italy'

Although most deponent verbs are intransitive, some of them such as __sequor__ 'I follow' can take a direct object:

- __hunc sequī sē iubet__ (Nepos)[[77]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-77)

'he ordered this man to follow him'

Deponent verbs are frequently used in their perfect participle form (e.g. __profectus__ 'having set out'):

- __mediā nocte profectus ad hostium castra māne pervēnit__[[78]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-78)

'after setting out at midnight, he reached the enemies' camp in the early morning'

## The subjunctive mood

As well as the indicative mood illustrated above, which is used for stating and asking facts, and an imperative mood, used for direct commands, Latin has a [subjunctive mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subjunctive_mood "Subjunctive mood"), used to express nuances of meaning such as "would", "could", "should", "may" etc. (The word _mood_ in a grammatical sense comes from the Latin _modus_, and has no connection with the other meaning of "mood", in the sense of "emotional state", which comes from a Germanic root.)[[79]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-79)

### Formation of the subjunctive

There are four tenses of the subjunctive, which in the verb __faciō__ are as follows:

- Present : __faciam__ (2nd person __faciās__) : 'I may do', 'I would do', 'I should do' (also simply 'I do')
- Imperfect : __facerem__ : 'I would be doing', 'I should do' (in a past context) (also simply 'I was doing')
- Perfect : __fēcerim__ : 'I have done', 'I did'
- Pluperfect : __fēcissem__ : 'I would or should have done' (also 'I had done')

The present subjunctive of 1st conjugation verbs ends in _-em_ instead of _-am_: __amem__ 'I may love, I would love'.

The present subjunctive of the verbs __sum__ 'I am', __possum__ 'I am able', __volō__ 'I want', __nōlō__ 'I don't want' and __mālō__ 'I prefer', ends in _-im_: __sim__ 'I may be, I would be', __possim__ 'I may be able', __velim__ 'I would like, I may wish', etc.

The imperfect subjunctive of every verb has the same form as the infinitive + _-m_: __essem__, __possem__, __vellem__, __amārem__, __vidērem__, __īrem__ etc.

### Uses of the subjunctive

The subjunctive has numerous uses, ranging from what potentially might be true to what the speaker wishes or commands should happen. It is often translated with "should", "could", "would", "may" and so on, but in certain contexts, for example indirect questions or after the conjunction __cum__ 'when' or 'since', it is translated as if it were an ordinary indicative verb.

Often in English the subjunctive can be translated by an infinitive; for example, __imperāvit ut īret__ (literally, 'he ordered that he go') becomes in more idiomatic English 'he ordered him to go'.

#### Potential subjunctive

The "potential" subjunctive is used when the speaker imagines what potentially may, might, would, or could happen in the present or future or might have happened in the past. The negative of this kind is __nōn__:[[80]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-80)

- __dūrum hoc fortasse videātur__ [[81]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-81)

'this may perhaps seem harsh'

- __quid si hoc fēcissem?__ [[82]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-82)

'what if I had done this?'

- __nōn facile dīxerim quicquam mē vīdisse pulchrius__ [[83]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-83)

'I couldn't easily say (= I don't think) that I have ever seen anything more beautiful'

#### Optative subjunctive

Another use is for what the speaker wishes may happen, or wishes had happened (the "optative" subjunctive). The negative of this kind is __nē__:[[84]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-84)

- __utinam iam adesset!__ [[85]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-85)

'if only he were here already!'

- __utinam ille omnīs sēcum suās cōpiās ēduxisset!__ [[86]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-86)

'if only he had taken out all his forces with him!'

#### Jussive subjunctive

It can also represent what the speaker commands or suggests should happen (the "jussive" subjunctive).[[87]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-87) The negative is again __nē__:

- __vīvāmus, mea Lesbia, atque amēmus__ [[88]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-88)

'let's live, my Lesbia, and let's love'

- __nē ... mortem timuerītis__ [[89]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-89)

'you should not fear death'

### In indirect statements and questions

Further information: [Latin indirect speech § Indirect questions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_indirect_speech#Indirect_questions "Latin indirect speech")

One important use of the subjunctive mood in Latin is to indicate that the words are quoted; this applies for example to subordinate clauses in indirect speech:[[90]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-90)

- __locum ubi esset facile inventūrōs__ (Nepos)[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:1-91)

'(he said that) they would easily find the place where he was'

It also applies to all indirect questions:

- __quārē id faciam, fortasse requīris__ ([Catullus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catullus "Catullus"))[[92]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-92)

'perhaps you ask why I do this'

When used in indirect speech or in an indirect question, the subjunctive is translated as if were the corresponding tense of the indicative.

### Subjunctive after conjunctions

The subjunctive mood is very frequently used in subordinate clauses following conjunctions.

#### After __cum__

Further information: [Temporal clause (Latin)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temporal_clause_\(Latin\) "Temporal clause (Latin)")

Used with the indicative, the conjunction __cum__ means 'at that time when', or 'whenever':[[93]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-93)

- __cum tacent, clāmant__ (Cicero)[[94]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-94)

'when they are silent, (it is as if) they are shouting'

Used with the subjunctive, however, it frequently means 'at a time when'.[[95]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-95) When __cum__ is used with the Imperfect subjunctive, a common way of translating it is 'while':

- __cum sedērem domī trīstis, accurrit Venerius__ (Cicero)[[96]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-96)

'while I was sitting sadly at home, Venerius suddenly came running up'

With the Pluperfect subjunctive, it often means 'after X happened':

- __cum excessisset Aegyptō Antiochus, lēgātī ... Cyprum nāvigant__ (Livy)[[97]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-97)

'after Antiochus had left Egypt, the ambassadors sailed to Cyprus'

It can also mean 'in view of the fact that' or 'since':[[98]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-98)

- __quae cum ita sint__[[99]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-99)

'in view of the fact that these things are so' / 'since this is so'

Another, less common, meaning is 'though':[[100]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-100)

- __nihil mē adiūvit, cum posset__ (Cicero)[[101]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-101)

'he did nothing to help me, though (or: at a time when) he could have done'

#### After __ut__

When followed by the indicative, the conjunction __ut__ can mean 'as' (e.g. __ut fit__ 'as generally happens') or 'as soon as' or 'when' (__ut vēnī__ 'as soon as I came'). But with the subjunctive __ut__ has the meaning 'that' or 'so that'.

It can represent purpose ('so that he could...'):[[102]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-102)

- __Crētam vēnit ut ibī quō sē cōnferret cōnsīderāret__ (Nepos)[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:3-103)

'(Hannibal) came to Crete so that there he consider (in order to consider) where he should go to next'

It can also be used to introduce an indirect command ('that he should...'):[[104]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-104)

- __imperāvit eī ut omnēs forēs aedificiī circumīret__ (Nepos)[[105]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-105)

'he ordered him to go round (lit. 'that he go round') all the doors of the building'

It can also represent result (making what is known as a "consecutive" clause):[[106]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-106)

- __idque sīc aedificāverat ut in omnibus partibus aedificiī exitūs habēret__ (Nepos)[[91]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:1-91)

'and he had built it in such a way that in all parts of the building it had exits'

Occasionally __ut__ with the subjunctive can mean 'although'.[[107]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-107)

#### After __sī__

Main article: [Latin conditional clauses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_conditional_clauses "Latin conditional clauses")

After __sī__ 'if', the subjunctive expresses an imagined or unreal situation:

- __quod, sī interfectus essem, accidere nōn potuisset__ (Cicero)[[108]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-108)

'which, if I had been killed, could not have happened'

- __sī revīvīscant et tēcum loquantur, quid respondēres?__ (Cicero)[[109]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-109)

'if they were to come back to life and talk to you, what answer would you be making?'

#### After __nē__

After _nē_ 'that not', the subjunctive can express a negative purpose:

- _hīnc nē exīre posset, ephorī valvās obstrūxērunt_ (Nepos)[[110]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-110)

'so that he would not be able to escape from here, the [ephors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephor "Ephor") blocked up the doors'

It can also introduce a negative indirect command:

- _Tīmoleōn ōrāvit omnēs nē id facerent_ (Nepos)[[111]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-111)

'Timoleon begged them all not to do this'

The conjunction _nē_ can also express a fear; in this case, the word 'not' must be omitted from the English translation:[[112]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-112)

- _verēns nē dēderētur_ (Nepos)[[103]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:3-103)

'fearing that he might be handed over to the enemy'

#### After __dum__

When used with the indicative, _dum_ means 'while' or 'as long as'. But when followed by the subjunctive, it often means 'until':[[113]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-113)

- __Verginius dum collēgam consuleret morātus (est)__ (Livy)[[114]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-114)

'Verginius waited until he had a chance to consult his colleague'

Another meaning is 'provided that':[[115]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-115)

- __ōderint dum metuant__ ([Accius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucius_Accius "Lucius Accius"))[[116]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-116)

'let them hate, provided that they fear'

#### After __priusquam__

The conjunctions __priusquam__ and __antequam__ both mean 'before (something happened)'. If the event actually happened, the verb is usually in the indicative mood; but when the meaning is 'before there was a chance for it to happen', the verb is subjunctive:[[117]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-117)

- __(collem) celeriter, priusquam ab adversāriīs sentiātur, commūnit__ (Caesar)[[118]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-118)

'he fortified the hill quickly, before it could be noticed by the enemies'

#### After __quīn__

The conjunction __quīn__ (literally, 'how should it not be?') is always used after a negative verb or the equivalent, typically 'there is no doubt that', 'who does not know that...?', and so on. The words following __quīn__ are always positive and usually state what was actually the case:[[119]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-119)

- __nōn dubitō quīn ad tē omnēs tuī scrīpserint__ (Cicero)[[120]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-120)

'I have no doubt that all your friends will have written to you'

- __quis ignōrat quīn tria Graecōrum genera sint?__ (Cicero)[[121]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-121)

'who does not know that there are three kinds of Greeks?'

Another usage is after a negative verb such as 'I can't help doing' or 'he did not refrain from doing':

- __facere nōn possum quīn ... tibī grātiās agam__ (Cicero)[[122]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-122)

'I can't do otherwise than to thank you'

- __Antiochus nōn sē tenuit quīn contrā suum doctōrem librum ēderet__ (Cicero)[[123]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-123)

'Antiochus did not refrain from publishing a book against his own teacher'

Equally it can be used in sentences of the kind 'A didn't happen without B also happening':

- __nūllum adhūc intermīsī diem quīn aliquid ad tē litterārum darem__ (Cicero)[[124]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-124)

'up to now I have not let a day go past without dropping you a line'

In sentences like the following, there is potential for confusion, since the _quīn_ clause, though positive in Latin, is translated in English with a negative:

- __nēmo fuit militum quīn vulnerārētur__ (Caesar)[[125]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-125)

'there was not one of the soldiers who was not wounded'

- __fierī nūllō modō poterat quīn Cleomenī parcerētur__ (Cicero)[[126]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-126)

'it was quite impossible that Cleomenes would not be spared'

In the following context, the words after __quīn__ express not what actually happened but what very nearly happened:

- __neque multum āfuit quīn castrīs expellerentur__ (Caesar)[[127]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-127)

'nor were they far from being expelled from the camp'

### Subjunctive after __quī__ 'who'

#### Generic

The pronoun __quī__ 'who' or 'which', when followed by a subjunctive, can mean 'a person such as' (generic):[[128]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-128)

- __quī modestē pārat, vidētur quī aliquandō imperet dignus esse__ (Cicero)[[129]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-129)

'he who obeys modestly, seems to be the sort of person who one day is worthy to rule'

#### Purpose

It can also mean 'in order to' (purpose):[[130]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-130)

- __lēgātōs Rōmam quī auxilium peterent mīsēre__ (Livy)[[131]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-131)

'they sent ambassadors to Rome to ask for help'

#### Explanatory

Another meaning is 'in view of the fact that' (giving an explanation), as in the following example, said jokingly of a [consul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consul "Consul") who was elected on the last day of the year:[[132]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-132)

- __fuit mīrificā vigilantiā, quī suō tōtō cōnsulātū somnum nōn vīderit__ (Cicero)[[133]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-133)

'(Caninius) was of amazing vigilance, in view of the fact that he didn't see any sleep in the whole of his consulate!'

#### Reported speech

Another reason for using the subjunctive after __quī__ is to show that the words of the __quī__ clause are quoted or part of indirect speech:[[134]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-134)

- __Paetus omnīs librōs quōs frāter suus relīquisset mihī dōnāvit__ (Cicero)[[135]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-135)

'Paetus made a gift to me of all the books which his brother had left him'

Clearly here Paetus had written or stated "I am giving you all the books which my brother left me", and Cicero is quoting his words indirectly to Atticus.

## The imperative mood

### Present imperative

The [imperative mood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperative_mood "Imperative mood") is used for giving direct orders. The active form can be made plural by adding _-te_:

- _dā mī bāsia mīlle, deinde centum!_ (Catullus)[[136]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-136)

'give me a thousand kisses, then a hundred!'

- _date dexterās fidemque!_ (Livy)[[137]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:8-137)

'give me your right hands and your oath!'

### Deponent imperative

[Deponent verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deponent_verb "Deponent verb") such as __proficīscor__ 'I set out' or __sequor__ 'I follow' have an imperative ending in _-re_ or -_minī_ (plural):

- _patent portae: proficīscere!_ (Cicero)[[138]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-138)

'the gates are open: depart!'

- _sequiminī mē hūc intrō ambae_ ([Terence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terence "Terence"))[[139]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-139)

'follow me this way inside, both of you'

### Passive imperative

The passive imperative is almost never found. It has the same endings as the deponent imperative:

- _neu bellī terrēre minīs_ (Virgil)[[140]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-140)

'and do not be terrified by threats of war!'

### Negative commands

An imperative is usually made negative by using __nōlī(te)__ (literally, 'be unwilling!') plus the infinitive. However, in poetry an imperative can sometimes be made negative with the particle _nē_:

- __nōlīte mīrārī__ ([Seneca the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Elder "Seneca the Elder"))[[141]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-141)

'don't be surprised'

- __nē mē terrēte timentem, obscēnae volucrēs!__ (Virgil)[[142]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-142)

'do not terrify me, who am already scared, obscene birds!'

A negative order can also use the perfect subjunctive:[[143]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-143)

- __dē mē nihil timuerīs__ (Cicero)[[144]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-144)

'do not be afraid on my account'

### Future imperative

Latin also has a future imperative or 2nd imperative,[[145]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:4-145) ending in _-tō_ (pl. _-tōte_), which is used to request someone to do something at a future time, or if something else happens first:

- _sī quid acciderit, ... scrībitō_ (Cicero)[[146]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-146)

'if anything happens, write to me'

This imperative is very common in early writers such as Plautus and Cato, but it is also found in later writers such as Martial:

- _ubi nōs lāverimus, sī volēs, lavātō_ (Terence)[[147]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-147)

'when we have finished washing, get washed if you wish'.

- _crūdam si edēs, in acētum intinguitō_ (Cato)[[148]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-148)

'if you eat it (cabbage) raw, dip it in vinegar.'

- _rīdētō multum quī tē, Sextille, cinaedum dīxerit et digitum porrigitō medium._ (Martial)[[149]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-149)

'Laugh loudly at anyone who calls you camp, Sextillus, and stick up your middle finger at him.'

Some verbs have only the second imperative, for example _scītō_ 'know', _mementō_ 'remember'.[[145]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:4-145)

- _nunc scītōte mē esse in summā sollicitūdine_ ([Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey"))[[150]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-150)

'know that I am now extremely anxious'

- _sed hoc mementōte_ (Cicero)[[151]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-151)

'but remember this'

### 3rd person imperative

A 3rd person imperative also ending in _-tō_, plural _-ntō_ exists in Latin. It is used in very formal contexts such as laws:

- __iūsta imperia suntō, īsque cīvēs ... pārentō__ (Cicero)[[152]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-152)

'orders must be just, and citizens must obey them'

### Other ways of expressing a command

Other requests are made with expressions such as __cūrā ut__ 'take care to...', __fac ut__ 'see to it that...' or __cavē nē__ 'be careful that you don't...'[[153]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-153)

- __cūrā ut valeās__ (Cicero)[[154]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-154)

'make sure you keep well'

The future indicative can be used for polite commands:[[155]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-155)

- __Pīliae salūtem dīcēs et Atticae__ (Cicero)[[156]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-156)

'will you please give my regards to Pilia and Attica?'

## The infinitive

Further information: [Latin tenses § The infinitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_tenses#The_infinitive "Latin tenses")

Although often referred to as a 'mood',[[157]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-157) the Latin infinitive is usually considered to be a verbal noun rather than a mood.[[158]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-158)

Latin has three infinitives in the active voice, and three passive. Since __faciō__ is irregular in the passive ('to be done' is __fierī__, taken from the verb __fīō__ 'I become'), they are here shown using the verb __capiō__ 'I capture':

Active:

- Present : __capere__ : 'to capture, to be capturing'
- Perfect : __cēpisse__ : 'to have captured'
- Future : __captūrus esse__ : 'to be going to capture'

Passive:

- Present : __capī__ : 'to be captured'
- Perfect : __captus esse__ : 'to have been captured'
- Future : __captum īrī__ : 'to be going to be captured'

The infinitives of __sum__ 'I am' are __esse__, __fuisse__, and __futūrus esse__ (often shortened to __fore__). __Possum__ 'I am able' has infinitives __posse__ and __potuisse__, and __volō__ 'I want' has __velle__ and __voluisse__. Neither of these verbs has a Future infinitive, and the Present infinitive is used instead.[[159]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-159)

The Future infinitive is used only for indirect statements (see below).[[160]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-160)

The passive Future infinitive is rare, and is frequently replaced with a phrase using __fore ut__.[[161]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-161)

Rarer tenses of the infinitive, for example _captus fore_ or _captūrus fuisse_, are sometimes found in indirect speech.

### Uses of the infinitive

The infinitive can be used as the subject, complement, or the object of a verb:[[162]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-162)

- __vīvere est cōgitāre__ (Cicero)[[163]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-163)

'to live is to think'

- __errāre, nescīre, dēcipī ... turpe dūcimus__ (Cicero)[[164]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-164)

'we consider to be in error, to be ignorant, to be deceived as something shameful'

### Prolative infinitive

It can also be used, as in English, dependent on an adjective, or with verbs such as __possum__ 'I am able' or __volō__ 'I want':

- __dulce et decōrum est prō patriā morī__ (Horace)[[165]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-165)

'it is a sweet and glorious thing to die for one's country'

- __nōn possum haec ferre__ (Cicero)[[166]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-166)

'I can't bear it'

It is likewise used, as in English, with verbs such as __iubeō__ 'I order', __vetō__ 'I forbid', __patior__ 'I allow', __volō__ 'I want' and so on, where the subject of the complement clause (sometimes mistakenly referred as an object) is in the accusative case:

- __volō tē hoc scīre__ (Cicero)[[167]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-167)

'I want you to know this'

However, other verbs of similar meaning, such as __imperō__ 'I order', __persuādeō__ 'I persuade', and __hortor__ 'I urge', are not used with an infinitive, but with __ut__ and the subjunctive mood:

- __hortātur mē ut senātūi scrībam__ (Cicero)[[168]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-168)

'he is urging me to write to the senate' (lit. 'that I should write')

### Historic infinitive

An infinitive is sometimes used to represent a series of repeated actions:[[169]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-169)

- _clāmāre omnēs_ (Cicero)[[170]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-170)

'everyone began shouting at once'

- _equitātus interim eōrum circum mūnītiōnēs Caesaris vagārī_ (Bellum Africanum)[[171]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-171)

'meanwhile the enemy cavalry kept on patrolling round Caesar's defences'

### Accusative and infinitive (indirect statement)

Further information: [Latin tenses § Indirect statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_tenses#Indirect_statement "Latin tenses"), and [Latin indirect speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_indirect_speech "Latin indirect speech")

A very common use of the infinitive in Latin, in which it differs from English, is its use for indirect statements, that is for sentences where a subordinate clause is dependent on a main verb meaning 'he says', 'he knows', 'he pretends', 'he believes', 'he thinks', 'he finds out' and so on. In Latin, instead of 'they pretend that they want', the idiom is to say 'they pretend themselves to want':

- __sē pācem velle simulant__ (Cicero)[[172]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-172)

'they pretend that they want peace'

Similarly 'I'm glad you've arrived safely' becomes 'I am glad you to have arrived safe':

- __salvom tē advēnisse gaudeō__ (Terence)[[173]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-173)

'I am glad you have arrived safely'

In this construction, the subject of the infinitive (__sē__, __tē__ in the above examples) is in the accusative case.

So common is this construction in Latin, that often the verb 'he said' is simply omitted if it is clear from the context, the accusative and infinitive alone making it clear that the statement is reported:

- __rem atrōcem incidisse__ (Livy)[[174]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-174)

'a terrible thing had happened (she said)'

The rule of tense in an accusative and infinitive construction is that the present infinitive is generally used for actions contemporary with the main verb, the perfect for actions which preceded it, and the future for actions which followed it. An example of the future infinitive using the future participle is the following:

- _Valerium hodiē audiēbam esse ventūrum_ (Cicero)[[175]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-175)

'I hear [epistolary imperfect] that Valerius is going to come today'

Often the _esse_ part of a future active or perfect passive infinitive is omitted:

- _frātrem interfectum audīvit_ (Seneca)[[176]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-176)

'he heard that his brother had been killed'

Less common is the periphrastic perfect infinitive, used when a potential pluperfect subjunctive is converted into an indirect statement:[[177]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-177)

- _Clōdium negant eō diē Rōmam, nisī dē Cȳrō audīsset, fuisse reditūrum_ (Cicero)[[178]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-178)

'they say that Clodius would not have returned to Rome that day, if he had not heard about Cyrus'

The above example also illustrates another feature of indirect statement, that a negative indirect statement ('they say that ... not') is usually represented by the use of the main verb _negō_ 'I deny'.

### Other ways of expressing 'that'

Not every subordinate clause which starts with the conjunction 'that' in English is translated with an accusative and infinitive. In some contexts __ut__ with the subjunctive is required, for example after a verb of happening:[[179]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-179)

- __accidit cāsū ut lēgātī Prūsiae Rōmae ... cēnārent__ (Nepos)[[180]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-180)

'it happened by chance that some ambassadors of King Prusias were dining in Rome'

In other circumstances a clause with _quod_ 'the fact that' is used with the indicative:[[181]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-181)

- __praetereō quod eam sibī domum dēlēgit__ (Cicero)[[182]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-182)

'I omit the fact that he chose that house for himself'

In less educated authors _quod_ could even substitute for the accusative an infinitive, though this did not become common until the second century:[[183]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-183)

- _lēgātī renūntiāvērunt quod Pompeium in potestāte habērent_ ([Caesar])[[184]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-184)

'the ambassadors reported that they had Pompey in their power'

This type of clause with __quod__ (which became __que__ in modern French, Portuguese and Spanish and __che__ in Italian) gradually took over from the Accusative and infinitive construction and became the usual way of expressing indirect speech in modern [Romance languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_languages "Romance languages") which are descended from Latin.

## Participles

Unlike Greek, Latin is deficient in participles, having only three, as follows:[[185]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-185)

- Present : _faciēns_ (pl. _facientēs_) : 'doing/making' or 'while doing/making'
- Perfect : _factus_ : 'done' or 'having been made'
- Future : _factūrus_ : 'going to do/make'

The Romans themselves[[186]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-186) considered the gerundive (see below) also to be a participle, but most modern grammars treat it as a separate part of speech:

- Gerundive : _faciendus_ : 'needing to be made'

There is no active perfect participle in most verbs, but in deponent verbs, the perfect participle is active in meaning, e.g. _profectus_, 'having set out'.

The verb _sum_ 'I am' has no present or perfect participle, but only the Future participle _futūrus_ 'going to be'. However the derived verb _absum_ 'I am absent' has a present participle _absēns_ 'absent'.

### Uses of participles

#### Adjectival participle

Participles have endings like those of adjectives, and occasionally they are used as though they were adjectives. If so, they refer to the state or condition that a thing or person is in:[[187]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-187)

- _aquā ferventī ... perfunditur_ (Cicero)[[188]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-188)

'he was doused with boiling water'

- _occīsōs sepelīvit_ ([Eutropius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eutropius_\(historian\) "Eutropius (historian)"))[[189]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-189)

'he buried the dead (those who had been killed)'

#### Participle as a verb

More frequently, however, a participle is more like a verb, and if one action follows another, it can often replace the first of two verbs in a sentence:

- _Caesar Cascae bracchium arreptum graphiō trāiēcit_ ([Suetonius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suetonius "Suetonius"))[[190]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:5-190)

'Caesar grabbed [Casca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servilius_Casca "Servilius Casca")'s arm and stabbed it with his writing instrument'

Literally, 'Caesar with writing instrument (_graphiō_) pierced (_trāiēcit_) for Casca (_Cascae_) the grabbed (_arreptum_) arm (_bracchium_)'

Participles can frequently be translated into English using a clause with 'when':

- _quaerentique viro 'satin salve?' 'minime!' inquit_.[[137]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:8-137)

'and when her husband asked "Are you all right?", she said "No!"

- _cōnātusque prōsilīre aliō vulnere tardātus est_ (Suetonius)[[190]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:5-190)

'and when he tried to leap forward he was slowed down (_tardātus_) by another wound'

'-ing' and 'who' are other possible translations:

- _currēns Lepta vēnit_ (Cicero)[[191]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-191)

'Lepta came running'

- _strīctō gladiō, ad dormientem Lucrētiam vēnit_ (Livy)[[192]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-192)

'drawing his sword, he came to Lucretia, when she was sleeping / who was sleeping'

Apart from 'when' and 'who', other translations are possible, such as 'if', 'since', or 'although':[[193]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-193)

- _oculus sē nōn vidēns, alia cernit_ (Cicero)[[194]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-194)

'although it can't see itself, the eye discerns other things'

A participle phrase can also stand for a noun clause, as in the following example:[[195]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-195)

- _captī oppidī signum ex mūrō tollunt_ (Livy)[[196]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-196)

'they raised a sign from the wall that the town had been captured' (lit. 'of the town having been captured')

Normally a Present participle represents an action which is simultaneous with the main event ('he came running'), and a Perfect participle represents one which has already happened ('after drawing his sword'). In the following example, however, the Perfect participle represents the result following the main action:

- _crīnīs scindit ... solūtōs_ (Virgil)[[197]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-197)

'she tore her hair, making it loose'

Participles are much commoner in Latin than in English. Sometimes multiple participles can be used in a single sentence:

- _noctū lūmine appositō experrēcta nūtrīx animadvertit puerum dormientem circumplicātum serpentis amplexū. quō aspectū exterrita clāmōrem sustulit._ (Cicero)[[198]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-198)

'in the night, in the light of a lamp placed nearby, the nurse, who had woken up, noticed that the boy, while he was sleeping, had been wrapped around with the coils of a snake; terrified by this sight, she raised a cry'

#### Ablative absolute

The phrase _strīctō gladiō_ (lit. 'with drawn sword') above is an example of a common idiom in which a noun and participle are put in the ablative case to represent the circumstances of the main event. This [absolute construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_construction "Absolute construction") in Latin is called an "ablative absolute" and is comparable to the Greek [genitive absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genitive_absolute "Genitive absolute") or the English [nominative absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominative_absolute "Nominative absolute").[[199]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-199) Other examples are:

- _in hostēs signō datō impetum fēcērunt_ (Caesar)[[200]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-200)

'when the signal was given (lit. 'with signal given'), they made an attack on the enemy'

- _at pater Aenēas, audītō nōmine Turnī, dēserit mūrōs_ (Virgil)[[201]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-201)

'but Father Aeneas, on hearing Turnus's name, immediately deserted the walls'

The present participle can also be used in an ablative absolute:

- _at illa audientibus nōbīs 'ego ipsa sum' inquit 'hīc hospita'_ (Cicero)[[202]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-202)

'but she, while we were listening, said "I am just a guest here myself!"'

- _nec imperante nec sciente nec praesente dominō_ (Cicero)[[203]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-203)

'without their master ordering it, or knowing, or even present'

The verb _sum_ ('I am') has no participle, except in the compound forms _absēns_ 'absent' and _praesēns_ 'present'. To make an ablative absolute with 'to be', the words are put in the ablative, and the verb is simply omitted:

- _puerulō mē_ (Nepos)[[204]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-204)

'when I was a little boy'

- _hīs cōnsulibus Fīdēnae obsessae, Crustumeria capta_ (Livy)[[205]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-205)

'when these men were consuls, Fidenae was besieged and Crustumeria captured'

## The gerundive

Further information: [Latin tenses § Periphrastic tenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_tenses#Periphrastic_tenses "Latin tenses")

The gerundive is a verbal adjective ending in _-ndus_ (_-nda_ etc. if feminine). It is usually passive in meaning (although a few [deponent verbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deponent_verb "Deponent verb") can form an active gerund, such as __secundus__ 'following' from __sequor__ 'I follow').[[206]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:6-206) The usual meaning of the gerundive is that it is necessary for something to be done. Often the word 'must' is a suitable translation:

- __nunc est bibendum__ ([Horace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horace "Horace"))[[207]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-207)

'now it is necessary to drink' (i.e. 'now we must celebrate')

- __Catō inexpiābilī odiō dēlendam esse Carthāginem ... prōnūntiābat__ ([Florus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florus "Florus"))[[208]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-208)

'[Cato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cato_the_Elder "Cato the Elder") with implacable hatred used to declare that [Carthage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carthage "Carthage") must be destroyed'

If a word is added to show by whom the action must be done, this word is put in the dative case (e.g. __nōbīs__ 'for us').[[206]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:6-206)

Because it is passive in meaning, the gerundive is usually formed from transitive verbs. However, intransitive verbs such as __eō__ 'I go' and __persuādeō__ 'I persuade', which can be used passively in an impersonal construction, can also have an impersonal gerundive, ending in _-um_:[[209]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:7-209)

- __mihī Arpīnum eundum est__ (Cicero)[[210]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-210)

'It is necessary for me to go to Arpinum' / 'I have to go to [Arpinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arpino "Arpino")'

- __persuādendum iūdicī est__ ([Quintilian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintilian "Quintilian"))[[211]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-211)

'the judge has to be persuaded'

The gerundive after _ad_ can also be used to express purpose (a use which it shares with the gerund, see below):[[212]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-212)

- __L. Septimium tribūnum militum ad interficiendum Pompeium mīsērunt__ (Caesar)[[213]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-213)

'they sent the military tribune Lucius Septimius to kill [Pompey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pompey "Pompey")'

- __hunc Dātamēs vīnctum ad rēgem dūcendum trādit Mithridātī__ (Nepos)[[214]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-214)

'[Datames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datames "Datames") handed this man over in chains to Mithridates for him to be led to the King'

## The gerund

The gerund is a verbal noun ending in _-ndum_ (accusative), _-ndī_ (genitive), or _-ndō_ (dative or ablative). Although identical in form to a neuter gerundive, and overlapping the gerundive in some of its uses, it is possible that it has a different origin.[[209]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:7-209)

Gerunds are usually formed from intransitive verbs,[[209]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-:7-209) and are mainly used in sentences such as the following where the meaning is 'by doing something', 'of doing something', or 'for the purpose of doing something'. A gerund is never used as the subject or direct object of a verb (the infinitive is used instead).

- _veniendō hūc exercitum servāstis_ (Livy)[[215]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-215)

'by coming here, you have saved the army'

- _aqua nitrōsa ūtilis est bibendō_ (Pliny the Elder)[[216]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-216)

'alkaline water is good for drinking'

- _idōneam ad nāvigandum tempestātem_ (Caesar)[[217]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-217)

'weather suitable (_idōneam_) for sailing'

- _sacrificandī causā, Delphōs ēscendī_ (Livy)[[218]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-218)

'for the sake of sacrificing, I climbed up to [Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delphi "Delphi")'

Occasionally a gerund can be made from a [transitive verb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_verb "Transitive verb") and can take a direct object:[[219]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-219)

- _subabsurda dīcendō rīsūs moventur_ (Cicero)[[220]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-220)

'by saying incongruous things laughs (_rīsūs_) are raised'

They can also be formed from deponent verbs such as _ingredior_ 'I enter':

- _aliīs timor hostium audāciam flūmen ingrediendī dedit_ (Livy)[[221]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-221)

'for others fear of the enemy gave them the boldness (_audāciam_) to enter (lit. of entering) the river'

However, if the verb is transitive, a phrase made of noun + gerundive is often substituted for the gerund:[[222]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-222)

- _lignum āridum māteria est idōnea ēliciendīs ignibus_ (Seneca)[[223]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-223)

'dry wood (_lignum_) is a suitable material for lighting fires'

## The supine

The supine is a rarely used part of the verb ending in _-tum_ or (in some verbs) _-sum_. When a verb is given in a dictionary with its four principal parts, such as _ferō, ferre, tulī, lātum_ 'I bring' or _mittō, mittere, mīsī, missum_ 'I send', the supine is the fourth part.

The supine is identical in form with the accusative case of 4th declension verbal nouns such as _adventus_ 'arrival', _mōtus_ 'movement', _reditus_ 'return', etc., but it differs from them in that it is a verb as well as a noun, and can sometimes take a direct object.

### Supine in -um

The supine is normally used to express purpose, when combined with a verb of movement such as _eō_ 'I go' or _mittō_ 'I send':

- _lūsum it Maecenās, dormītum ego Vergiliusque_ (Horace)[[224]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-224)

'[Maecenas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maecenas "Maecenas") went to play a game, Virgil and I to sleep'

- _spectātum veniunt, veniunt spectentur ut ipsae_ (Ovid)[[225]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-225)

'(the girls) come to watch, but they also come so that they can be looked at themselves'

In the following example it takes a direct object:

- _lēgātōs ad Caesarem mittunt rogātum auxilium_ (Caesar)[[226]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-226)

'they sent ambassadors to Caesar in order to ask for help'

The accusative of the supine is also used to make the rare future passive infinitive, for example, _captum īrī_ 'to be going to be captured', which can be used in indirect statements referring to the future (see above):[[227]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-227)

- _ante reditum eius negōtium cōnfectum īrī putō_ (Cicero)[[228]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-228)

'I think the business will be completed before his return'

### Supine in -ū

There is another form of the supine, an Ablative in _-ū_, found with certain verbs only. But this cannot take an object.[[229]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-229) It is used in phrases such as _mīrābile dictū_ 'amazing to say', _facile factū_ 'easy to do':[[230]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-230)

- _dictū quam rē facilius est_ (Livy)[[231]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_syntax#cite_note-231)

'it is easier in the saying than in reality'
